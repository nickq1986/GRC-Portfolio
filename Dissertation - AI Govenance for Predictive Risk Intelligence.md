## Abstract

*Predictive Risk Intelligence is an AI use case currently deployed across various domains to forecast potential risks before they occur. At the heart of its predictive engine, is a confluence of machine learning and big data technologies referred to as smart information systems. Optimal utilization of this use case is expected to bring benefits to organisations and contribute to the drive towards sustainable development goals outlined by the United Nations. However, these advantages are hindered by system threats and vulnerabilities which are accompanied with ethical implications. There is a limited corpus of empirical academic work in this particular field that related to the governance of PRI and the wider applications of SIS. It is the aim of this paper to review the state of the art and contribute to empirical findings to evaluate how ready organizations are to integrate PRI as a driver towards SDGs whilst mitigating the ethical implications. *

**Keywords**: Predictive Risk intelligence, Smart Information Systems, Artificial Intelligence, Machine Learning, Big Data.

## Introduction

### 1.1 Contextualisation

Predictive risk intelligence (PRI) using Smart Information Systems (SIS) is an Artificial Intelligence (AI) use case adopted across several domains, that leverages advanced technologies and data analytics to identify, assess, and forecast potential risks. SIS are a broad technological concept which describe the combination of AI and advanced Big Data Analytics (BDA) to collect, process and analyse large and complex datasets, from various sources, in an intelligent and automated manner.  uses the term "SIS" to categorize Socio-Technical Systems (STS) that leverage a specific AI technique called Machine Learning (ML) often employing artificial neural networks, to extract insights from vast quantities of typically unstructured data. STS are a concept that denotes the design of a system that optimises the confluence of social arrangements and technical components . The interplay between SIS components is evident in activities like sentiment analysis and trend prediction, where vast amounts of data generated by STS are processed by ML algorithms, to augment and automate delineated tasks such as predictive modelling and sentiment classification . Akin to similar emerging technologies, the discourse surrounding the employment of SIS, is often directed towards the issue of managing and optimizing the consequences of innovation. Central to this discussion are the human rights affected by SIS as expressed United Nation’s (UN) Universal Declaration of Human Rights and their implementation by the attainment of actionable and measurable Sustainable Development Goals (SDG’s) . To ensure that AI systems work towards SDG’s, requires a carefully co-ordinated global collaborative effort involving the use of dynamic and agile policy frameworks to safeguard and guide their implementation .

#### Privacy, Transparency and Accountability

As the confluence of advanced ML algorithm such as DNN’s and big data becomes increasingly reliant on large training datasets, the implications individual rights for privacy become concerning when they contain PII . ### 4.1 Theoretical Foundation

The research for this study focused on the intersection of a framework of processes and controls for managing the principles of organisational Information Governance (IG), Risk, and Compliance and the PRI use case. In study context, the principle of IG aligns with  concept of cyber-security (CS) governance; as a set of policies, procedures and guidelines to achieve the organisations objectives and protect digital assets. Across domains such as CS and Information Security (IS), organisations may choose to adopt governance best practises that meet the requirements laid out by standards to successfully achieve and demonstrate policy compliance . Risk Management (RM) best practices are fundamental to IG policy, therefore provide the foundation for standards such as the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 27001, and National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Cybersecurity Framework (CSF),  . This is particularly relevant to organizations that adopt PRI as this reports previous sections have identified system threat and vulnerabilities that pose significant risks to organisations adopting this use case, potentially with implications for wider society. Policy should be aligned with a wider demonstration of compliance with the organisation's contractual, ethical, and regulatory obligations. This may take the form of various hard and soft regulatory instruments, . Hard regulation includes the provisions from instruments such as the GDPR and the newly in force EU AI act, which although span the EU jurisdiction, have set an international regulatory precedent . Soft regulation refers to guidance such as the IEEE 700 series of standards of transparency of autonomous systems and the Principles for the Ethical Use of Artificial Intelligence in the United Nations System . These ethical principle, represent a synthesis of commonly agreed upon shared values within wider international AI discourse , often forming the basis for both hard and soft regulation. Whilst these, principles are widely adopted, it is argued that they are theoretical in nature, addressing the question of ‘*what*’, which means that the next logical question relates to ‘*how*’ these concepts are implemented, a question addressed through the exploration of tangible governance processes , and ‘*where’ *the benchmark is for which the effectiveness of these are measured, which is widely discussed in the previous sections SLR as through the attainment of measurable SDG’s. ### 4.3 Research Method

#### 4.3.1 Philosophical Approach

There are several valid philosophical approaches to this study such as positivism, post-positivism and interpretivism. After careful consideration it was determined that this study should proceed with an interpretivist philosophy. This was motivated by the ontological metaphysical interpretation that the mechanisms of governance, risk and compliance are grounded in relativism. This is because it is believed that governance processes, perceptions of risk and at times, of laws and regulations are grounded in societal, cultural and historical phenomena which are subjective, open to interpretation from multiple perspectives and changeable throughout the course of time . The epistemological approach of this study is inductive and assumes that diverse multiple perceptions play a pivotal role in the processes that govern AI. It assumes that as a relatively new phenomena in the context of governance, that there will be perceptive variance in how this AI use case can be leveraged to achieve organizational objectives, the risks that AI poses to those objectives and the selection of appropriate technical and organisational controls requires to manage such risks to meet compliance requirements.

#### 4.3.2 Research Method

A mixed methods approach was taken through the generation of a questions framework to conduct qualitative research using semi-structured interviews. It was determined that the former would enhance the validity of the research by presenting consistent and relevant questions framework, however the latter was preferable over a deductive approach due to scarcity of predefined theories in the existing literature, therefore it was hoped that the inductive approach from which varying subjective observations are derived , would enable a contribution of new theories. The rationale for semi structured interviews was largely due to suitability to qualitative research, enabling the SLR to inform a general guide, whilst at the same time, allowing flexibility for the researcher to adapt the questions to the flow of the interview accordingly . This would allow for adjustments to be made for various domains and levels of expertise. Semi-structured interviews are less often used for exploration between two or more variables in both inductive and deductive research . Therefore, it was hoped that this method would facilitating deeper and additional insights into the themes already addressed by the SLR in relation to the ethical implications of PRI and SIS, but not expected. Semi-structured interviews are often used for explaining and evaluating the relationship between two or more variables  therefore it was expected that this method would explain how the organisation is addressing ethical concerns and using PRI to drive SDG 9. By using the ISO/IEC 42001 as a benchmark, the researcher could evaluate as how well organisation were implementing processes such as oversight, data management and risk management to handle these challenges.

Interview Questions were extracted from a content analysis of the ISO/IEC 42001 standard obtained from the BSI website. ### 6.2 Organisational Governance Gaps

The lack of maturity in AI/ML specific policies for the integration of PRI technologies, is significant because this demonstrates deviance between that the state of current practice and the guidance outlined in the ISO-IEC 38507 model adaption and as implemented by alignment with the ISO/IEC 42001 standard. The implications of this suggest that organisations who lack a comprehensive AI policy, or a policy that does not align courses of action with the specifications of the use case risk non-compliance or unwanted outcomes unforeseen by policies tailored to legacy system (ISO-IEC 38507, p. 11).The findings from the least mature organisations substantiated this by demonstrating that lack of clear policy objectives has had a cascading effect leading to further deviance from ISO/IEC 38507 guidance and ISO/IEC 42001 standard best practice, by contributing to structural and operational siloing, ultimately resulting in a complete failure to sustain PRI integration. Despite most organisations with higher levels of maturity having a policy in place, it was observed that most were perceived as high-level documents for responsible AI use, with ongoing discussions tailor policy objectives to specific PRI tools. These responses corroborate the concerned of GRC professionals interviewed throughout the course of the current study who perceive that current state of high-level AI policies to be insufficient for the PRI use case which required policies that outline clearly defined parameters, roles, actionable standard operating procedures and embedded controls to be effective. This also overlaps the findings of previous research that strongly suggests that the application of high-level principles outlined in the fields of AI ethics by researchers such as , currently lack clear implementation inter alia clear governance rules, guidelines, processes, practices and technological tools to ensure these principles are met in practice .

This sentiment of the ‘what’ should be done at being a high level not really being addressed by the tangible ‘how’,  is reflected by the responses to the questions in relation to employment of PRI tools in alignment with SDG 9, as this was overwhelmingly viewed sceptically throughout the sample, with most of the participants stating this this was not considered seriously, suggesting limited conscious progress toward the attainment of SDG 9. However, his study was conducted within a broader scope, therefore imposing a limited focus on how organisations align governance objectives towards SDG 9 attainment within the context of the PRI use case. Future research could adopt a narrower scope on organizations that integrate PRI technologies with an explicit contribution to SDG 9 attainment in their policy objectives. The limited understanding of SDG 9 alignment revealed by the current study proposes that such research endeavour would be invaluable for two reasons. Firstly, the findings suggest that scepticism around SDG attainment lies grounded in what is perceived by most as a course of action lacking in return on investment (ROE). Therefore, by focusing on organisations who actively pursue attainment, my offer an alternate perspective, thereby offering ROE incentives to drive SDGs. Secondly, this may reveal deeper insights into the policies, procedures, guidelines and processes that effectively align PRI with SDG 9 attainment, thus contributing to a wider and more comprehensive framework for responsible use.

### 6.3 Barriers for PRI integration

This study has provided evidence of organisations who have placed considerable investment in the development PRI technologies being unable to overcome human, technological, and organizational to sustain operational integration. This confirms the finding of  Artificial Intelligence Sector Study which also identified limited expertise, skills gaps, transparency concerns, data access limitations, quality concerns, compliance issues, as well as insufficient investment in research and development (R&D) and challenges to wider AI adoption. This is significant because the latter and the current study have used separate varying methodologies to derive similar findings, thus enhancing the validity of the current research. The current study has also shown that internal organisational barriers currently across industries which stem from a lack of consideration prior to PRI integration, whereby leadership has not clearly established the ROI metrics associated with the use case. Instead, organizations are adopting AI/ML technologies driven by a perceived urgency to remain competitive, referred to by some of the study sample as ‘Fear of missing out’ or ‘FOMO’. The implications for organisations adopting this hasty approach, are investments may be concentrated on outsources technical expertise, who do not fully understand the wider business context, and cannot be fully transparent with the remaining stakeholders, including strategic decision makers who lack the necessary education to interpret technical explanations and integrate them with wider strategy. The lack of investment of R&D, are that organizations who use PRI tools, will for the foreseeable future, by reliant on procurement. Once of the biggest implication of this, are these organizations are vulnerable to what is an increasingly growing societal concern of supply chain risks, including data supply chain disruptions and SIS data poisoning through widescale misinformation, as well as external barriers, such as the inability to outsource third parties for data handling, otherwise risk in compliance violations. The biggest external barrier came from the compliance requirements of the GDPR, which had implications such as embedding provisions such as the right to be forgotten into model development and organisations such as those in UK healthcare who had compliance issues with cross-border data flows when outsourcing third party data handlers.  However, there was minimal discussion surrounding the implications of the anticipated provisions of the European Union's (EU) Artificial Intelligence (AI) Act (2024). This is likely due to the study limitations, whereby the sample was restricted to organisations who operated within the UK. However, as the EU AI act (2024) comes increasingly into effect with a broader, risk-based focus the AI/ML system robustness, transparency, human oversight , it is likely that this will have affect EU cross-border organisations. Therefore, future research that investigates the added implications for UK organisations who affectively consider the provisions of the EU Act (2024) throughout the deployment of PRI tools would be valuable as this act becomes a significant consideration in the wider regulatory landscape.

### 6.4 The Role of leadership

Despite the barriers to PRI integration, the value of this use case was perceived as profoundly capable of enabling a competitive advantage, largely due to the ability of SIS to derive large data sets from STS, thus giving organisations the ability to make actionable decisions in real-time. However, it was commonly agreed that leadership played the most significant role in addressing the barriers, as well as communicating the value of PRI to internal and external stakeholders whilst addressing concerns. The implications of this, are that organisations require top-down and cross-generational approach to address the of lack of expertise and workforce education as a precursor to stakeholder communication. This is consistent with  study which finds that the developing tailored programs that enhance cognitive abilities is effective at bridging gaps when integrating AI into a multigenerational workforce. Upskilling of the entire workforce, was seen by a number of participant key to addressing the most prominent concerns over automation replacing human labour, resulting in job-loss. The main argument for leadership to take an active role in addressing these aspects of PRI integration, is that leadership was a catalyst, having the for responsibility for culture change. However, the role of government was also raised throughout numerous responses related to addressing stakeholder concerns, which was disregarded to the limitations of the scope of this report which aimed to focus on the organisation’s perspective. The UK regulatory approach has been developed to foster innovation by reducing regulatory restrictions  . Therefore, future research in how organisations perceive the role of current levels of government intervention in shaping the innovation would be valuable for regulatory bodies targets courses of action to have a positive impact on PRI and wider SIS adoption.

### 6.5 The Need for Greater Organisational Robustness

This theme confirmed the findings of the previous sections SLR, as well as introduced several new societal concerns such as the increasing threats to the AI/ML and data supply chains, as well as the use of Deepfakes as a vector for misinformation-based data poisoning. This has multiple implications from a security perspective; however, the most significant common sub-theme was the perception lack of public trust had a negative impact of PRI, SIS. This is especially valid since the UK has adopted a regulatory approach to foster innovation of AI/ML technologies, because the UK National AI Strategy explicitly acknowledges that public trust forms the basis for future investments in the AI/ML ecosystem . The qualitative findings of the Alan Turing report that informed the UK national strategy has indicated that negative public perception, real or perceived, is likely to significantly impact the adoption of emerging technologies  . This factor largely overlaps with the role of leadership because it the sample responses of both the Alan Turing report and the current study emphasise the need for showcasing successful AI/ML applications and use cases and engaging in positive public dialogue through media and internal organisational channels to counter concerns such as job displacements, surveillance, and broad AI issues, such as super-intelligence, which was commonly referred to by this current sample as a ‘Skynet’ scenario. Internal transparency concerns were largely based on the inability for stakeholders to understand the algorithms, with was largely viewed as a human centric issue. However, the current study found that the technological aspect of transparency was largely overlooked by the participants, with only one participant referring to alignment with explainability frameworks as a control measure to make their algorithms more transparent. The role of Explainable AI (XIA) has gained traction in the research field of AI ethics in recent years.  exploration of this technical solution to make complex ML based intrusion detection more interpretable to cyber analysts demonstrate how XIA can be used across various applications for enabling transparency towards stakeholders. The significance of this technology is reinforced by combined legislation such as DPA (2018) and the Equality Act (2010) which stipulate that satisfactory level of transparency is required to protect lawful human rights, this should be enabled by explainable systems, which are interpretable by those subject to the algorithms to ensure fair outcomes . The findings of the current study are consistent with   systematic mapping study which suggests that although there is a sharp incline in recent years in research relating to the development of technical solutions for applications of XIA, there is a lack of understanding of how XIA is implemented at an organisational level and integrated into processes and workflows. However, this was only observed as a finding through the current study’s thematic analysis and due to the limitations of the study scope, there was no real investigation as to why this gap currently exists. Therefore, future research into the organisational/managerial perspective of XIA remains a worthy research endeavour. In terms of managing the issues directly related to ML tools used for PRI identified in the previous sections SLR, in was apparent number of organizations maintained a security posture inherited from legacy systems, albeit with consideration to mandatory data protection impact assessments for PRI tools.  This approach focused on applying appropriate controls commonly associated with information security frameworks such as the ISO/IEC 27001 and NIST CSF such as data loss prevention, access controls, multifactor authentication, continuous monitoring, and antimalware solutions to comply with data protection and industry specific regulations. However, most noted that this approach was insufficiently tailored to address the challenges unique to PRI integration which included a higher degree of reputational damage, flawed decision making and wider ethical implication through misuse of SIS. This was arguably the most significant finding because in addition to the absence of clearly defined processes such oversight, it was apparent that risk management processes were divergent from ISO/IEC 42001 best practices with the absence of impact assessment and risk treatment options for the AI/ML and incomplete data management processes as evidenced by the lack of mechanism such  the development secure data supply chains by most of the study sample. In relation the research objectives of this study, this suggests that when the ISO/IEC 42001 is used as a benchmark to evaluate the organisational readiness for PRI integration, the organisations interviewed in the current study are lacking a structured and holistic organisational approach that considers both the technical aspects of AI and its broader societal concerns. However, it should be noted that the study sample size was restricted to n=13 participants, primarily based in the UK. Whilst this is was sufficient to achieve data saturation, it is important to acknowledge the potential limitations of generalizability. Another important consideration is that the ISO/IEC 42001 is a newly developed standard for AI management and none of the participants interviewed throughout this study had stated that their respective organizations had aligned there best practices the standard requirements. There is also limited empirical research the time of writing the current study in relation to the effectiveness of the ISO/IEC 42001 due its infancy, with recent literature consisting primarily of content analysis , which is subjective in nature. Therefore, as the ISO/IEC becomes more prominent, further research should be carried out across wider samples and across various regions to evaluate the effectiveness of AI management system compliance. This would be valuable for organisations looking to adopt best practices across various regulatory jurisdiction including the UK and regions where the provisions of the EU AI act are applicable.

### Validity of Research

The ability to critically appraise sources is fundamental for evidence-based research and practice. Enabling the various stakeholders of this report to ability to evaluate the validity of the study findings is imperative to significantly improving PRI integration for organizations. The current study is assessed according to the most commonly used evaluation criteria for qualitative research as purported by  as credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability. The credibility of this research is determined by the invariance between the authors interpretation of the problem statement as derived from relevant sources such as the IS0/IEC 24030 (2024) in relation to supporting literature presented in the previous sections SLR and the views expressed by the study sample as represented in the framework analysis. Dependability was achieved by the mixed methods with deductive approach to the formulation of the questions framework to maintain a consistent line of questioning for interview participants, as well as an inductive approach to analysis to mitigate bias in the emergence of thematic findings. As demonstrated, in the discussion section, many of the themes addressed through the data synthesis support their findings of independent research, which supported the confirmability of this research by indicating that alternative methods will yield similar results, strengthening many of the arguments made throughout the study. However, despite the supporting similarities to other study findings, to the best of the authors knowledge this is the first study, to understand the GRC processes adopted, to integrate PRI technologies driven by SIS, contributing to empirical findings for the corpus of wider AI/ML research. However, one major limitation to this research lies with the nature of qualitative research which is prone to academic bias. Due to the constraints of the authors dissertation requirements, all forms of collaboration and outsourcing were prohibited, thus the coding and thematic analysis was subject to the authors individual bias of interpretation. In order to mitigate this, the author has used standardised and transparent methods, which may be easily reproduced by future researchers, and it is suggested that future research of a similar nature is carried out by collaborative research projects to address evaluate organisational readiness to integrate PRI, as well as any of the other research agendas discussed in the previous section.

## Conclusion

### 7.1 Summary

In the light of the complexities surrounding PRI use case, this research has aimed to contribute to the wider corpus of knowledge by answering the research question: *How ready are organisations for integrating PRI and managing the ethical implications of SIS technologies? *To answer this question the research carried out the objective of adopting the newly published ISO/IEC 42001 standard as a benchmark to assess the maturity and preparedness of corporate approaches to GRC for organisations that use PRI and whether they were consciously aligning its usage with SDG 9.This objective was achieved by firstly adopting a deductive approach consisting of a content analysis of the ISO/IEC 42001 with pre-defined thematic framework derived from the ISO/IEC 38500:2015 theoretical model for governance of IT and codes generated from the systematic review of the existing corpus. With the questions matrix generated to ensure relevance to all aspects of the PRI use case, an inductive approach was used by using this theoretical framework to conduct semi-structured interviews across a sample size of n=13 participants.

### 7.2 Contribution and limitations

The analysis of the interview transcripts found that there was an emergence significant themes, which achieved its research aims by contributing to the corpuses empirical evidence, gaining a deeper understanding of GRC processes used for PRI, identifying governance gaps and emphasising the need for greater levels of organisational robustness to mitigate ethical concerns. This highlighted social, ethical and legal implications for policymakers and researchers which was largely addressed in alignment with the stated action plan in section 6.1 by outlining future directions for researchers. Many of the future research agendas were set the limitations of the current study, many of which were determined by the project scope and selected participation sample. Discussions surrounding the integration of PRI in alignment with SDG’s were not substantial enough to make practical recommendations for the achievement of SDG 9. This was largely due to the scepticism of the sample size with regard to how seriously organisations considered SDGs as a relevant issue. There was also limited discussion in relation to the implications of the EU AI act, which was largely due to the sample being primarily based in the UK. The report was focused on governance processes at the organisational level, meaning that many of the issues raised by the sample such as the role of government and regulators in building public trust were outside of the study scope. At the time of writing the current study, the ISO/IEC 42001:2023 is a newly developed standard, meaning that although this standard was used in the current study as a benchmark to evaluate organizational readiness for PRI adoption it should be noted that evaluation of the effectiveness of the standard itself as a framework for AI governance is itself in its infancy. The greatest limitation of the current study lies in the overarching biases of thematic coding/analysis. This is commonly mitigated through iterative rounds of coding whereby independent researchers agree on codes to generate themes. However, constraints of the current study were determined by dissertation requirements meaning that no independent researchers could be outsourced for the purpose of collaborative coding.

### 7.3 Final Recommendations

In conclusion and in alignment with the stated action plan (section 6.1) this report makes the following final recommendations for policy makers based off the study findings:

**Establish a Use Case**: Prior to making any substantial investments in PRI adoption, organizations should assess current business processes to identify where AI/ML and big data technologies will improve existing gaps. Prioritisation of use-case should be assessed on where adoption can deliver a return on investment that meets stakeholder expectation. This can be aided by use of the ISO/IEC TR 24030 (published as ISO/IEC 24030:2024 at the time of this study), which provides detailed information including technical information, data requirements, stakeholder expectations, KPI’s for various AI/ML applications across various domains. **Policy Development**: Policy should align with the specific tools and intended purpose to avoid hi-level principle-based policies. Policies must clearly define roles and responsibilities, internal controls and standard operating procedures that ensure responsible use. **Process**: Policy should inform processes for regular auditing, stakeholder management, upskilling of the workforce including leadership, regular staff training. Risk management should be informed by policy, including risk treatment options and controls tailored to the PRI use-case to prevent deviation from the intended use of the system and the organisations objectives. Impact assessments should extend beyond mandatory data protection impact assessments, to include comprehensive PRI specific impact assessments that follow the requirements laid out by the ISO/IEC 42001 standard to assess the impacts of AI systems on individuals, groups and societies affected throughout the life cycle.  **Roles and Responsibilities: **Organisations should leverage tools such as the RACI matrix (responsible, accountable, consulted, and informed) to ensure accountability and avoid oversight gaps. Responsibilities should be divided into development, including data scientists and ethics specialists, and operations, including operations engineers and cyber-security specialists, however these cross functional teams must adopt mechanisms to collaborate effectively. Oversight teams should include legal, data protection, cyber security and ethics functions, capable of carrying out regular audits and impact assessments. However, it should be clearly stated that ultimate responsibility for overseeing and auditing policy adherence rests with top level-management and the organisation should remain accountable when any third parties are involved throughout the system life cycle, being prepared to allocate responsibilities between the partners, suppliers and external stakeholders. **Fairness: **The fairness of algorithmic outcomes should be viewed as an ongoing process, involving regular review and clearly defined processes to update the systems to align with expectations of individuals, groups and societies. This should be achieved by close collaboration between policy makers and AI development experts to address concerns that stem from algorithmic bias incorporating and continuously monitoring fairness metrics. These could include individual fairness metrics that assess whether similar individuals are treated similarly by an AI system and demographic parity metrics, which assess whether the proportion of individuals from different demographic groups who receive a positive outcome from an AI system is equal .

### Final Reflection

Leadership should be pro-active, in addressing negative connotations associated with PRI. This is achieved through the guiding principles of Fairness, Accountability and transparency (FAT theorem) . However, leadership must demonstrate the ‘how’ over ‘what. The recurring themes of training, and education must be rectified to demonstrate that organisations embrace ethical use PRI and are accountable for the responsible use of SIS. Investment should be targeted to develop the expertise to ensure models are developed produce accurate and unbiased outputs, and with the intent integrating XIA integration to mitigate algorithmic opacity.  Algorithmic opacity at the organisation or micro-level has significantly contributed to PRI failure, through a subsequent lack of transparency, resulting in loss of trust. Therefore, it is argued that leaders should play particular attention to the role of XIA in demonstrating ‘how’ the high level ‘what’ is being effectively applied in practise.
